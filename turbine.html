Research · Mixed-integer programming

Optimisation of Maintenance Planning for Offshore Wind Turbines

Offshore turbines fail expensively and are reached slowly. The maintenance decision is bound less by the machine than by the boat.

Type Group projectInstitution CentraleSupélecDate November – December 2025Tools Pyomo, Gurobi
MILPMaintenance SchedulingReliabilityPyomoHiGHS
§ 1

The problem

An offshore wind farm must plan maintenance across a fleet of turbines whose condition deteriorates over time. Preventive maintenance is cheaper per intervention but spends resource on turbines that had not yet failed. Corrective maintenance is triggered by failure and carries an outage penalty for every hour the turbine is down.

What makes the offshore case distinct is access. Crews are finite, vessels take time to travel between turbines, and weather restricts work to defined maintenance windows. A schedule that is optimal for the turbines alone may be infeasible for the vessel.

§ 2

Why it matters

Operations and maintenance is one of the largest lifetime cost components of an offshore wind asset, and much of it is logistics rather than engineering. Treating maintenance as a scheduling problem under travel and crew constraints, rather than as a per-turbine reliability rule, is where the cost actually sits.

§ 3

Model

A mixed-integer linear programme schedules preventive and corrective interventions across turbines and time periods. Turbine condition is represented through discrete health states, so degradation enters the model without requiring a continuous stochastic process. The objective minimises total lifecycle cost across preventive maintenance, corrective maintenance, vessel transport and outage penalties.

§ 4

Scale and implementation

A 30 week planning horizon under crew capacity, a limit of one farm per crew per week, and a one week travel lockout on any transfer between farms. Formulated in Pyomo and solved with HiGHS to proven optimality on small, medium and large instances.

§ 5

Results

The optimum was zero intervention on all three instances. Rather than accept that as a modelling failure, we traced it: at 75€ per repair including transport against 2€ per week of downtime, preventive action only pays back after 37.5 weeks. The horizon is 30 weeks. The break-even sits beyond the end of the model.

The given cost structure therefore makes the problem ill posed for preventive maintenance. No schedule can be optimal because no intervention can recover its own cost inside the window.

A negative result, and the useful part of the project. Diagnosing why an optimum is degenerate is worth more to an operator than a schedule produced from cost parameters that cannot support one.

§ 6

Summary

The result an operator can use is not the schedule but the sensitivity: how the preventive threshold should move as failure rates are revised, and how much of the benefit depends on vessel availability rather than on maintenance policy itself.

Group project. Model formulation, implementation and diagnosis carried out collaboratively. Pyomo with the HiGHS solver.
